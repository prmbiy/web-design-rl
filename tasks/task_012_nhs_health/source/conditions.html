Overview — Type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is a common condition that causes the level of sugar (glucose) in the blood to become too high. It's caused by problems with a chemical in the body called insulin.

It's often linked to being overweight or inactive, or having a family history of type 2 diabetes. Without treatment, it can cause serious problems with your eyes, heart and nerves.

What is HbA1c?

HbA1c (sometimes called glycated haemoglobin) is a measure of your average blood glucose level over the past 2 to 3 months. A blood test result of 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) or above usually means you have type 2 diabetes.

Blood glucose is the main sugar found in your blood and your body's primary source of energy.

Symptoms

Many people have type 2 diabetes without realising. This is because symptoms do not necessarily make you feel unwell.

Symptoms of type 2 diabetes include:

  • peeing more than usual, especially at night
  • feeling thirsty all the time
  • feeling very tired
  • losing weight without trying to
  • itching around your penis or vagina, or repeatedly getting thrush
  • cuts or wounds taking longer to heal
  • blurred vision
Urgent advice: Contact 111 if:
  • you have symptoms of type 2 diabetes
  • your blood sugar level is consistently higher than 11 mmol/L
  • you have signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), such as feeling or being sick, deep or fast breathing, breath that smells fruity, or feeling very drowsy

DKA is a serious problem that needs urgent treatment in hospital.

Causes

Type 2 diabetes happens when your body does not make enough insulin, or the insulin it makes does not work properly. This is known as insulin resistance.

You're more at risk of developing type 2 diabetes if you:

  • are over 40 (or 25 for south Asian people)
  • have a close relative with diabetes (such as a parent, brother or sister)
  • are overweight or obese
  • are of Asian, African-Caribbean or black African origin (even if you were born in the UK)

Getting diagnosed

See a GP if you have any of the symptoms of type 2 diabetes, or you're worried you may have a higher risk of getting it.

You'll need a blood test, which you may have to go to your local health centre for if it cannot be done at your GP surgery.

The Diabetes UK risk tool

You can use the Diabetes UK Know Your Risk tool to check your risk of type 2 diabetes. It only takes a few minutes and could be the most important thing you do today.

Treatment

There's no cure for type 2 diabetes, but treatment can help keep your blood sugar levels under control.

Treatment usually involves:

  • healthy eating — eating a wide range of foods, including some carbohydrate, fruit and vegetables
  • regular physical activity
  • losing weight if you're overweight
  • medicine — usually tablets such as metformin

Your GP may refer you to the NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme if you're at high risk of developing type 2 diabetes. The programme can help you make lasting lifestyle changes.

Important

If you're prescribed metformin, do not stop taking it without speaking to a doctor first — even if you feel better. Stopping your medicine suddenly can cause your blood sugar to rise.

Living with Type 2 diabetes

Managing type 2 diabetes is mainly down to you. These daily steps can help you stay well:

  1. Eat a balanced diet — include vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, beans, pulses, nuts, fish, eggs and dairy. Cut down on sugar, salt and red or processed meat.
  2. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity every week — such as brisk walking, swimming or cycling.
  3. Take your medicine exactly as prescribed and never skip doses.
  4. Check your feet every day for cuts, blisters or numbness, and report any changes to your GP or podiatrist.
  5. Attend your annual diabetic eye screening appointment to check for diabetic retinopathy.
  6. Stop smoking if you smoke — ask your GP about free NHS Stop Smoking support.
Call 999 if you or someone you know is unconscious or having a seizure

Severe low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia) or DKA can be life-threatening and need emergency treatment.
